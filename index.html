What we actually do

Three jobs. Everything else is a detail inside one of them.

1. Evaluate and overhaul the network

Walk the floor. Count what is connected. See what fails.

That means workstations, printers, phones, Wi-Fi, switches, firewall, shared storage, backups. How traffic actually moves. What is consumer gear pretending to be business gear. What remote access exists, and whether it is a hole.

Then rebuild the parts that are fragile: addressing, Wi-Fi that covers the space, staff vs guest when it earns its keep, a firewall that belongs in a shop, remote access that is deliberate. Write it down so the next person is not guessing.

2. Streamline the workflow and the environment

The network is only half of why a shop fights its computers.

Shared folders and permissions that match how people actually work. One place for files instead of five. Print, scan, and phone paths that do not require tribal knowledge. Email and logins a 4–15 person shop can live with. Staged PC refresh instead of panic buys. Moving off an aging desktop-as-server, an ISP gateway, or a tool nobody can administer — when the shop is ready, not as a religion.

You leave with a short runbook: how backups run, who is admin, what to do when X fails.

3. Stabilize — then you run it, or we keep a light hand

Once it is quiet, pick an end state. None of these is “call us every time Outlook blinks.”

Hand-off. You run it from the runbook. We are available for the next planned upgrade.

Scheduled tune-ups. We come back on a cadence and check backups, firewall, and drift.

Small retainer. We keep an eye on a shop that is already stable, because the footprint is small — not because we want a 200-seat desk.

Desktop tickets after that are the exception, not the product.

How an engagement runs

No prototype theater. No 40-page binder. A shop-sized plan, then the work.

1

Discovery and environment audit

On-site walkthrough of the Austin-area shop. What is connected, what breaks, who does what, where the files live, whether the backup has ever been restored on purpose. You get written findings: what is fragile, what is overbuilt, what to leave alone.

2

Stabilization plan

A short, priced plan. Order of work: stop the bleeding, clean the network, fix workflow friction, document. You choose the end state on paper — self-manage, scheduled tune-ups, or a small retainer.

3

Overhaul and transition

The work happens in a window the shop can live with. Firewall, Wi-Fi, file path, identity, printers and phones, staged hardware if it is time. The one or two people who will touch the system day to day get shown how, not handed a mystery.

4

Hand-off or quiet management

Packet: diagram, admin list, backup proof, “if this fails, do that.” Then you run it, we return on a schedule, or we keep a light retainer on a shop that is already quiet.

What a typical shop looks like

Starting point

About eight computers. One aging desktop acting like a server. The ISP’s gateway running the whole network. Wi-Fi that dies in the back. Files in three places. A backup that has never been tested. Someone on staff who “knows the computers” and is tired of it.

Ending point

A firewall that belongs there. Wi-Fi that covers the floor. One file location with permissions that match the work. Printers and phones on a path people can repeat. A backup that has been restored on purpose. A one-page runbook. Either the owner runs it, or we check in because it is quiet enough to check in on.
